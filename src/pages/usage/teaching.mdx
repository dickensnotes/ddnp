##  Sample Assignments and Activities

This section of _Dickens Notes_ features assignments and activities from instructors who have taught with attention to Dickens’s working notes and serial practice. Each practical assignment or activity is introduced by a short essay providing a critical framework for its pedagogical approach.

_Do you teach with Dickens’s working notes?_ Submit your activity or assignment with an accompanying short essay for consideration at dickensnotes@gmail.com.

## Why Teach with _Dickens Notes_?

Given their unwieldy length, Dickens novels can present unique challenges in the classroom. Students often struggle to keep track of vast character networks, multiple narrative threads, and the complexities of serial form. The working notes offer a unique means of addressing and accessing this textual complexity. In as little as 19 pages they can function as an index to the form and content of an 800-page text. Incorporated into formal assignments or used as a reference point for discussions, they help students grasp Dickens’s compositional practice, the constraints and affordances of serial form, and the movement of plot and character over multiple installments. 

Recent scholarship on teaching Victorian literature has championed approaches that help students experience what it meant to be a Victorian reader or writer of serial fiction. While some instructors simulate Victorian serial consumption via the serial assignment of reading across the course of a semester (Tange), others experiment with having students become part of reading communities (Cook and Henley), or imagine the composition of _Edwin Drood’_s unfinished parts (Chavez and Hauhart). 

The approach made possible by the working notes is unique in its use of these short texts as windows into a novel’s serial form and composition. Each page of notes can become an access point to understanding what Daniel Siegel has called the “architectural” nature of a single installment–its shape, pacing, handling of characters, and relationship to other installments and the novel as a whole. Students can use the notes to reconsider the novel’s plot development, character networks, and serial structure. And, by engaging with texts kept as an author’s personal records, students can debate the value of taking authorial intention and compositional process into account when interpreting a literary text. 

The _Dickens Notes_ project’s legible, spatially faithful, easy-to-navigate transcriptions of the working notes allow students to engage with these texts in much more nuanced ways than the transcriptions in the appendix of a popular press edition can allow. As a tool for practicing close reading, these transcriptions can help students engage in interpretations that go beyond language to consider space, non-textual markings, and evidence of compositional decision-making: placement on the page, ink color, emphases, questions and later answers, deletions, and emendations. The project’s introductions and annotations offer further material to aid–but not determine–students’ interpretive engagements with the notes. 

Dickens Notes is one among many online resources available to instructors teaching Dickens’s novels. Other great resources include:

- [Project Boz](https://digital.wpi.edu/collections/sq87bv394) allows students to view the original serial parts of Dickens's novels.

- [Dickens Journals Online](https://www.djo.org.uk/) allows students to read the weekly novels _Hard Times_ (1854), _A Tale of Two Cities_ (1859), and _Great Expectations_ (1860–61) in the context of their original publication in _Household Words_ and _All the Year Round_, which also contain essays by Dickens and provide insight into his editorial practice.

- The [_Reading Like a Victorian_](https://readinglikeavictorian.osu.edu/) website provides a timeline to help instructors interested in teaching Dickens alongside other nineteenth-century novels published serially to identify contemporaneous publications.

- The [_Dickens Letters Project_](https://dickensletters.com/) provides online access to many of Dickens's letters.
